## Definition

**Dissociation** (Czech: *disociace*) is a clinical phenomenon characterized by disruption of the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, body representation, and motor control. The DSM-5 classifies dissociative disorders as a distinct category encompassing **dissociative amnesia**, **depersonalization/derealization disorder**, and **dissociative identity disorder (DID)**, with subclinical dissociative experiences widely distributed across trauma-spectrum and stress-related conditions.

Dissociation operates along a continuum from normative experiences (mild absorption, highway hypnosis) through trauma-protective responses (peritraumatic depersonalization) to chronic structural conditions involving identity fragmentation. The dominant theoretical frame — the **structural dissociation theory** of van der Hart, Nijenhuis, and Steele — distinguishes apparently-normal personality (ANP) modes from emotional personality (EP) modes, with chronic complex trauma producing increasingly elaborated divisions between them.

Phenomenologically, dissociation presents as: **depersonalization** (felt unreality of self, "watching from outside"), **derealization** (felt unreality of environment, "as if behind glass"), **dissociative amnesia** (gaps in autobiographical memory inconsistent with ordinary forgetting), **identity alteration** (subjective discontinuities in self), and **somatic dissociation** (anesthesia, motor symptoms without organic basis).

Dissociation is **adaptive in origin** — it evolved as a freeze-system response to inescapable threat — but becomes pathological when it persists beyond the eliciting context, generalizes inappropriately, or fragments the integrative capacity required for ordinary cognitive and relational functioning.

## Context

Within the Prismatic Platform Kompendium, dissociation is critical to **differential diagnosis** — the discipline of distinguishing trauma-induced fragmentation from systematically induced epistemic annihilation. The two presentations can resemble each other superficially but require radically different interventions:

- **Trauma-origin dissociation** is a defensive response to overwhelming events; treatment involves stabilization, trauma processing (EMDR, prolonged exposure, narrative exposure therapy), and integration. The person's epistemic apparatus is **intact but compartmentalized**.
- **EA-induced fragmentation** is the active destruction of epistemic capacity by an external agent; treatment requires extraction from the epistemic environment, reconstruction of source-evaluation skills, and rebuilding of metacognitive trust. The apparatus itself is **damaged, not merely partitioned**.

Misdiagnosis runs in both directions and produces [Iatrogenic Harm](@/glossary/iatrogenic-harm.md): treating EA victims with trauma-integration protocols can re-traumatize and reinforce the perpetrator's framing; treating dissociative trauma survivors as EA victims pathologizes adaptive responses and can trigger destabilization.

- The Kompendium chapter [/kompendium/06-klinicke-aplikace/](@/kompendium/06-klinicke-aplikace/_index.md) provides a differential-diagnosis matrix.
- [Mereologická rekonstrukce](@/glossary/mereologicka-rekonstrukce.md) describes the whole-part reintegration approach used in both conditions but with different sequencing.
- [Narrative Identity](@/glossary/narrative-identity.md) is structurally damaged in both presentations but through different mechanisms.
- The {{ cross_link(path="/hub/dd/decisions/", text="Decision Engine") }} does not pathologize dissociated testimony but flags it for context-sensitive integration: gaps and contradictions in dissociative reporting carry different evidentiary weight than in non-dissociative testimony.
- [Metakognitivní destrukce](@/glossary/metakognitivni-destrukce.md) is a distinct EA mechanism that may co-occur with dissociation but is not reducible to it.
