## Definition

**Iatrogenic harm** (Greek *iatros* = "healer" + *-genic* = "produced by") denotes harm caused by the therapeutic intervention itself rather than by the underlying condition. The term originated in medicine to designate complications of treatment — surgical infections, drug side effects, hospital-acquired conditions — and has been extended to psychotherapy and psychological interventions, where it includes:

- **Retraumatization** — therapeutic exposure that re-injures rather than processes
- **False-memory induction** — confident belief in events that did not occur, generated by suggestive interview techniques (the recovered-memory controversies of the 1980s–1990s being the canonical case)
- **Symptom proliferation** — therapy frames that produce the symptoms they purport to discover (ironic example: certain DID treatment protocols can multiply alters)
- **Dependency** — therapeutic alliance that erodes autonomous coping
- **Misdiagnosis cascade** — initial diagnostic error producing escalating mismatched interventions
- **Stigma and labeling** — diagnostic classification producing identity-internalization harms

Empirically, psychotherapy is generally net-beneficial across populations, but iatrogenic harm rates are non-trivial: meta-analytic estimates suggest 5–10% of clients experience deterioration during therapy, with higher rates for specific high-risk modalities and populations. The discipline of *evidence-based practice* exists in significant part to constrain iatrogenic risk through protocolized intervention.

In the **EA recovery context**, iatrogenic risks are especially elevated because the underlying damage is precisely to the epistemic apparatus the therapy must rely on. Standard trauma-processing techniques can:

- Reinforce the perpetrator's framing if the therapist accepts the victim's perpetrator-supplied narrative uncritically
- Destabilize an already-fragile self-structure through premature intensive exposure
- Substitute the therapist's interpretive authority for the perpetrator's, perpetuating epistemic dependence rather than restoring autonomy
- Mistake EA-induced fragmentation for trauma dissociation and apply protocols that miss the active external manipulation still in progress

## Context

Within the Prismatic Platform Kompendium, iatrogenic harm is treated as a **first-class clinical concern** in the application chapter [/kompendium/06-klinicke-aplikace/](@/kompendium/06-klinicke-aplikace/_index.md). The chapter explicitly warns clinicians against several common errors:

- **Premature narrative reconstruction** — pushing the client toward a coherent story before the epistemic apparatus is repaired enough to evaluate competing narratives
- **Interpretive authority transfer** — replacing the perpetrator as "the one who knows what really happened" rather than restoring the client's own evaluative capacity
- **Diagnosis-as-identity** — anchoring the client's self-understanding to a clinical label in ways that produce new fragility
- **Single-modality fixation** — applying one therapeutic frame (CBT, EMDR, IFS) without integration

The [Mereologická rekonstrukce](@/glossary/mereologicka-rekonstrukce.md) approach is designed in part to **minimize iatrogenic risk** by sequencing interventions: first restore source-evaluation capacity, then re-establish narrative coherence, then process specific traumatic content — never the reverse.

- [Disociace](@/glossary/disociace.md) and EA must be carefully distinguished; treating one as the other produces reliable iatrogenic harm.
- [Narrative Identity](@/glossary/narrative-identity.md) reconstruction must be **client-led** rather than therapist-imposed.
- The {{ cross_link(path="/hub/dd/decisions/", text="Decision Engine") }} supports clinicians by surfacing differential-diagnosis indicators rather than committing to single hypotheses prematurely — a design pattern that mirrors good clinical practice.
- [Color Teams](@/glossary/color-teams.md) Blue Team protocols include iatrogenic-risk audits when designing platform interventions: even technical defenses against EA can produce iatrogenic effects (e.g., aggressive content filtering inducing learned helplessness or reality-checking-dependence).

The kompendium's stance is unequivocal: an EA recovery intervention that **does not actively monitor for iatrogenic harm** is professional malpractice, not therapeutic care.
